# Today's contribution: Programmable Horizons

Long Horizon Agents Build Day · AGI House · August 22, 2026

## The inversion

Most work on long-horizon agents focuses on memory: how an individual agent can
retain more of its past across sessions and rollouts.

I approached the theme from the opposite direction:

> **The Horizon already exists. We can make its programming observable—and
> eventually programmable.**

The Horizon is not only memory inside an AI agent. It is the shared, forward-facing
option space inherited by future human and machine agents. States, companies,
institutions, communities, and technical systems already modify that option space
every day.

## What programs a Horizon?

The fundamental building blocks are not AI agents alone. They include:

- capital and financing;
- talent, education, research, and learning culture;
- compute, chips, data centres, energy, and network infrastructure;
- data, standards, legal categories, and technical benchmarks;
- laws, treaties, institutions, permissions, and defaults;
- human organisations and coordinated human-agent networks;
- AI models and autonomous agents as one increasingly important component.

These resources and rules determine what future actors will be able, required, or
forbidden to do. They are therefore programmable elements of the future.

## How the programming is represented

Sovereign Lens treats a public action as a **write event** against one or more of
eight Horizon registers:

1. Timers — activation dates, review periods, and sunsets.
2. Commitments — treaties, contracts, constitutional constraints, and durable plans.
3. Parameters — thresholds, budgets, quotas, and other values that reweight outcomes.
4. Defaults — what happens if nobody acts again.
5. Permissions — who may access, operate, modify, export, or withdraw a capability.
6. Priors — curricula, corpora, benchmarks, standards, and inherited assumptions.
7. Capacities — capital, talent, compute, infrastructure, energy, and institutions.
8. Categories — the definitions future policy and technical systems execute against.

Each observation should record the actor, register, action, activation date,
binding strength, reversibility, affected jurisdiction and capability, source,
and uncertainty. Together, those writes form a versioned trajectory rather than
a stream of disconnected news.

## Why sovereignty is the first lens

State actors are actively programming sovereign AI capability now. Infrastructure
agreements, export permissions, cloud regions, training programmes, public funding,
model-access rules, evaluation institutions, and technical standards can increase
capability while simultaneously changing dependency, control, and optionality.

That is why I built **Sovereign Lens**: an evidence-first instrument for observing
how those writes reshape sovereignty over time.

It does not decide whether an initiative is “pro-US,” “pro-China,” “pro-UN,” or
“pro-Africa.” It preserves the evidence and asks:

- What capability changed?
- Who owns or controls it?
- Which dependency increased or decreased?
- Can the recipient operate, evaluate, replace, or withdraw independently?
- Which future options opened, closed, or became conditional?

## Why multiple models

One model provides one situated interpretation. Sovereign Lens gives multiple
models the same evidence, question, graph snapshot, and output structure. It then
preserves consensus, disagreement, assumptions, missing evidence, and confidence
instead of averaging them into one authoritative answer.

The purpose is not to pretend that models are national delegates. It is to use
their divergence as analytical parallax: one viewpoint sees direction; several
viewpoints can reveal depth. The current hackathon prototype demonstrates this
interface with deterministic perspective fixtures; authenticated provider runs
remain clearly marked as the next implementation step.

## Short participant-channel version

I approached Long Horizon Agents Build Day from a different direction. Most teams
are extending an agent's memory—giving it a longer past. My premise is that **the
Horizon already exists, and it is already being programmed**.

The Horizon is a shared, forward-facing object shaped by capital, talent, compute,
energy, infrastructure, culture, data, standards, institutions, human networks,
and AI agents. State actors are actively writing to it through investments,
treaties, export permissions, infrastructure, training, regulation, and technical
standards.

My contribution today is **Programmable Horizons**, an approach for representing
those changes as observable, versioned write events. The instrument is
**Sovereign Lens**—an open-source, evidence-first system for watching how those
writes change sovereign capability, dependency, control, and optionality.

To reduce dependence on any one worldview, the same evidence is examined through
multiple model perspectives. Agreement is useful; disagreement is preserved as
parallax. The system then returns later to compare interpretation with outcome.

**Everyone is extending the agent's past. I am making the programmable future
observable.**
