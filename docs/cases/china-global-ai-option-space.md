# China global AI diplomacy and receiving-state option space

## Source

Arindrajit Basu, “China's Pivot on Global AI,” Carnegie Endowment for
International Peace, May 21, 2026:
https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/05/chinas-pivot-on-global-ai

Source role: secondary analysis. It supports attribution of Carnegie's argument;
it does not independently establish downstream lock-in, state intent, or a net
sovereignty effect in any receiving country.

## Carnegie trajectory

The article describes China's AI diplomacy as moving from infrastructure and
associated technical standards toward a broader attempt to shape rules, norms,
institutions, capacity-building, UN processes, and the proposed World AI
Cooperation Organization. It also states that Global South countries have not
given wholesale normative support and continue engaging several leading powers.

## Sovereign Lens transformation

```text
Infrastructure → capability → standards → trained personnel → institutions
→ governance vocabulary → future option space
```

The evaluative question is not simply which external actor gained influence:

> Does the receiving state retain the practical capacity to evaluate, combine,
> govern, replace, and exit the available technology stacks?

Claims about infrastructure, resolutions, programs, institutions, and agreements
must be corroborated with the primary sources linked by Carnegie. Effects on
switching cost, interoperability, local evaluation capacity, and operating rights
require separate country-level evidence.
